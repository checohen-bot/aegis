# Founder Principles

These are the non-negotiables. They are written down because unwritten principles erode — first by exception, then by habit, then entirely. Every one of these has a price, and we pay it knowingly. When some future decision seems to demand violating one of them, the correct reading is that the decision is wrong, not the principle. If a principle here ever genuinely must change, it changes by explicit amendment to this document — never by quiet drift.

## 1. Business first, always

We analyze businesses, not tickers; we serve investors, not traffic. Every analysis in the product starts from the operating reality of a company — economics, competitive position, capital allocation — because that is where durable returns actually come from. And the same principle governs how we build: features are justified by whether they make users better investors, not by engagement graphs. In investing software, engagement-maximization is user harm with a dashboard. A feature that increases activity while degrading reasoning is a defect. We will always be able to say, in one sentence of plain language, how any feature strengthens at least one pillar of the belief system. If we can't, we don't build it.

## 2. Decision quality is the metric that governs

When metrics conflict — growth versus quality, engagement versus reasoning, speed versus rigor — decision quality wins. This is the north star made operational: we would rather have a smaller number of users making measurably better decisions than a large number making faster ones. Companies become what they measure, and we refuse to become a company that measures the wrong thing brilliantly.

## 3. Model-agnostic, permanently

AI capabilities are permanent; specific models are replaceable infrastructure. GPT, Claude, Gemini, whatever comes next — these are components, chosen per task on merit and swapped without ceremony when merit changes. No Aegis capability may depend on the idiosyncrasies of one vendor's model, and no vendor's roadmap may become our roadmap. The frontier will change hands repeatedly during this company's life; our architecture assumes it. The corollary binds product too: our moat must never be "we wrap the best model," because that moat evaporates on someone else's release day. Our moat is the reasoning discipline, the evidence architecture, and the accumulated memory — things no model swap can take away.

## 4. Auditability is a precondition, not a feature

Every recommendation explainable. Every thesis evidenced. Every decision reconstructible: what was known, what was reasoned, what was decided, and why — recoverable years later, exactly as it was. This binds engineering (data models must preserve reasoning provenance from day one, because auditability cannot be retrofitted), product (no black-box output ships, ever), and culture (our own decisions get written down with their reasoning, because a company selling institutional memory had better practice it). If we cannot show our work, we do not ship the work.

## 5. Trust is the asset; never spend it

Trust compounds slower than any other asset we hold and is destroyed faster. Therefore: no dark patterns, no manufactured urgency, no incentive structure that profits from user behavior we know to be harmful, no confidence theater — stated certainty must track actual certainty, and "we don't know" is a complete sentence we will use in production. Every monetization decision passes one test: *would a fully informed user endorse this?* If the honest answer is no, the revenue is a loan against the mission, taken at ruinous interest. We don't borrow against the mission.

## 6. Long-term maintainability over short-term velocity

We are building a decades-scale company whose product custodizes decades of user reasoning. Code, data models, and institutional knowledge must be built for the engineer who inherits them in year eight. We will take the slower path that stays comprehensible over the faster path that becomes archaeology. Shortcuts are sometimes rational — but at Aegis they are taken explicitly, documented, and scheduled for repayment, never slipped in silently. Velocity that mortgages the future isn't speed; it's a payday loan, and the platform of record for someone's financial reasoning does not run on payday loans.

## 7. Depth before breadth

One customer (the individual investor), one asset class (public equities), one broker integration (IBKR), one platform (web-first) — each done with total rigor before any expansion. Every premature broadening divides the attention that rigor requires, and rigor is the product. Expansion is earned by demonstrated excellence in the current scope, never by restlessness, competitor moves, or investor enthusiasm. The graveyard of fintech is full of platforms that went wide before they went deep.

## 8. Simplicity is engineered, not skipped

Apple-level simplicity in experience, Palantir-level rigor underneath — and the second is what makes the first honest. Simplicity achieved by omitting rigor is a toy; rigor delivered without simplicity is abandoned. The hard, expensive work of this company is doing both: absorbing complexity inside the system so the investor experiences clarity, while keeping every layer of depth reachable for whoever asks "why?" When we simplify, we compress the path to understanding. We never amputate the understanding itself.

## 9. We are participants in our own system

Aegis's discipline applies to Aegis. Major internal decisions get a thesis, evidence, and a written record. Post-mortems judge our process, not just our outcomes. We change course when evidence demands and hold course when only noise does. A company cannot sell a standard of reasoning it does not live; users will eventually smell the difference, and they will be right to.

These principles will cost us: deals we won't sign, growth we won't chase, demos we won't fake, quarters that look slower than competitors'. That cost is not an unfortunate side effect of the strategy. It *is* the strategy. Anyone can hold principles that are free.
